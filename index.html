Do prediction markets know the odds?

A contract that pays $1 if something happens and trades at 30¢ is a market saying "30% likely". If markets are right, things priced at 30% should happen about 30% of the time. This page checks that on every settled Kalshi market with enough trading, and shows the statistics behind the answer.

Could you profit from the gaps?

Average return on every market in a price group if you had bought at the price shown and held to the end, after Kalshi's fee. A known pattern in betting markets, the favourite–longshot bias, is that long shots win less often than their price says, so buying "no" on them pays. If the interval includes 0%, the result could be luck. Prices are the bid/ask mid: a real buyer pays the ask, a little more, so real returns are lower than shown.

Which way are prices biased?

One number per group: the slope of a line fitted through the calibration chart, on the log-odds scale. A slope of 1 means calibrated. Below 1, prices are too extreme: favourites win less often and long shots more often than priced. Above 1, prices are too timid and should be further from 50%. If the interval includes 1, there's no clear bias. Categories need at least 200 markets.

Do prices react fast enough?

If a price takes in news fully, how it got there shouldn't matter: markets that rose over the last week should resolve "yes" exactly as often as their price says, just like markets that fell. A positive gap for risers (and a negative one for fallers) means prices under-react and keep drifting the same way; the opposite means they overshoot. Uses the prices 7 days and 1 day before close. Reaction within hours of news would need hourly prices for every market, which this site doesn't collect.

Market vs. a weather forecast

An outside check on the market. Kalshi lists daily high-temperature brackets for seven US cities (New York, Chicago, Miami, Austin, Denver, Los Angeles, Philadelphia). For each one we also compute a probability from a weather forecast: the forecast high issued the day before (Open-Meteo), plus how far off that forecast was for the city over the previous 90 days. Both are compared with the official high the market settled on, using the market price at about midnight before the day. These markets are all ≥ 10k contracts; the filters above don't apply here.

Market vs. a volatility model

A second outside check, on Kalshi's markets for where the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 will close. The model starts from the last close before the market price was read and assumes the index moves as much as it did over the previous 60 trading days (a lognormal spread with no drift). It ignores news and the fat tails real markets have, so the market should beat it; the question is by how much. Kalshi's Bitcoin and Ether price markets open less than a day before they close, so they have no 1-day price and aren't covered.

How this compares

Brier.fyi (formerly Calibration City) is the broadest tracker: Kalshi, Polymarket, Manifold and Metaculus, hundreds of thousands of markets, letter grades, and the same question compared across platforms. Use it to compare platforms. Exchanges also publish their own accuracy figures, such as "95% accurate", which usually count how often the favourite won and leave out cancelled markets.

This site covers one exchange and asks a narrower question: how sure can we be?

  • Every score comes with a 95% interval, and calibration with a significance test, so you can tell a real pattern from luck.
  • Markets in one event are resampled together, so a 20-candidate race doesn't count as 20 independent tests.
  • Skill is measured against simply guessing how often "yes" happens, not just against a coin flip.
  • The same markets are scored at 1, 7 and 30 days, by volume and over time.
  • Returns after fees show whether a gap could actually be traded, and the bias and reaction tests say which way prices are off.
  • Outside benchmarks (a weather forecast and a volatility model) check the market against probabilities that don't come from traders.

How to read the numbers

Brier score
The average of (price − outcome)², where the outcome is 1 or 0. 0 is perfect; always saying 50% scores 0.25. Lower is better.
Skill vs. base rate
1 − Brier ÷ the Brier of always forecasting the overall share of "yes" outcomes. 0 means no better than that dumb forecast, 1 is perfect. It shows whether prices carry information, not just whether they're calibrated.
Calibration error (ECE)
The average gap, in percentage points, between the price and how often it happened, weighted by how many markets each group has.
Calibration test
Spiegelhalter's z test. Its p-value is the chance of a gap this large if prices were exactly right. Below 0.05 means the miscalibration is unlikely to be luck. It treats markets as independent, which overstates certainty (see limits).
95% intervals
Per price group: Wilson interval on the observed frequency. For Brier and skill: a bootstrap that resamples whole events, because markets in one event are linked.

Method and limits

  • Source: Kalshi's public API, settled binary markets closing since January 2025, open at least a day, with at least 10,000 contracts traded (100,000 for sports). Updated daily.
  • The price is the mid of the best bid and ask on the daily candle ending at least N days before close, or the last trade if the spread is wider than 10¢. Daily candles make "1 day" anywhere from 1 to 2 days.
  • Markets in one event (say, each candidate in an election) are not independent: exactly one wins. The bootstrap handles this; the per-group intervals and the z test do not.
  • Kalshi lists many sports and crypto markets, so they dominate "All". To keep the data small, only a fixed 1-in-10 sample of sports events is kept (whole events, picked by ticker, not by outcome). Filter by category to compare like with like.
  • Trading costs fees and the bid/ask spread, so a gap from the dashed line is not automatically a profitable bet.
